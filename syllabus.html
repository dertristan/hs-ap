Syllabus

Course description

What does it mean for a democracy to be “polarized”? This seminar examines polarization as both a contested concept and a political phenomenon, with particular attention to the role of group identity in shaping democratic conflict.

We begin by unpacking what polarization actually refers to, ideological, social, affective, and why these distinctions matter. The course then narrows to affective polarization and the social-identity foundations of partisan division, before turning to how partisan divides interact with other identity cleavages such as religion or ethnicity. Empirical work from Europe and beyond grounds the discussion throughout.

Course objectives

The seminar is built around reading political science carefully rather than accumulating results. By the end of it you should be able to take a claim about polarization, work out what it is a claim about, and say what evidence would support or undermine it.

Three things run through every session. The first is conceptual discipline: polarization means several different things, and most disagreements in this literature are about which one is meant. The second is measurement: an affective polarization score is a construction, and what it is built from decides what it can support. The third is comparison: almost all of this literature was built on the United States, and whether it describes European multi-party democracies is an open empirical question rather than an assumption.

The term paper is where you do this on a question of your own.

General information

Tip

Prerequisites

Two different things go by this name, and it is worth keeping them apart.

Formally, the prerequisite depends on which of the two modules in Section 3.3 you register this seminar under. Whether you meet it is a question for the module catalogue and the Studienbüro, not for me.

In terms of what this course assumes, no prior work on polarization, party systems or political psychology is expected. The readings are research articles rather than textbook chapters, so you should be comfortable reading one, but learning how to read one is part of what the seminar is for.

What is required of you

Assessment has two parts, and the vocabulary is the examination regulations’ own. Coursework (Studienleistung) is assessed pass or fail, and passing it is a prerequisite for the graded work. The term paper (Prüfungsleistung) carries the mark.

Coursework, pass or fail

1. Active participation (Mitarbeit). Section 13b of the examination regulations allows participation to be set as coursework in a discussion-based course of this size, and it is set here. It is assessed on the whole of your contributions across the term, not on any single session. What counts is that you engage with the reading and with what other people say about it.

2. Doing the reading. Each session lists core reading, which everyone does, and optional reading, which is there for anyone who wants to go further and as a starting point for term papers. The core reading is mandatory rather than recommended. It is also the basis on which participation is assessed, because a discussion of a text nobody has read is not a discussion.

3. A term paper idea presentation (Referat). You pitch your term paper to the group in session 11 or 12. A few minutes on your question, why it is worth answering, and how you would answer it, followed by discussion. The point is to get criticism while there is still time to act on it.

Term paper, graded

  • Form: a written term paper (schriftliche Hausarbeit).
  • Length: at most 7000 words, the limit set in the module catalogue.
  • Deadline: 15 January, 23:59.
  • Topic: your own, on something the seminar covers. You pitch it in session 11 or 12, and session 13 is optional individual consultation.

A workable paper takes a claim from the literature and does something specific to it: tests it against a case or a dataset it was not built on, takes apart a concept two authors are using differently, or works out what a measurement choice does to a published conclusion. A summary of what several authors have said is not a term paper.

Registration and withdrawal

Registration for the Prüfungsleistung, deregistration, and the applicable deadlines are handled by the Studienbüro under the examination regulations, not by me. Check the dates there and keep to them, because responsibility for meeting them sits with you.

If a good reason, illness in particular, prevents you from submitting the term paper on time, section 30 of the examination regulations applies. You must submit a written application to the Studienbüro without delay, with evidence. The examination board decides, and the burden of proof is on the applicant. Tell me as well, but telling me is not a substitute for the formal application.

Section 5 additionally allows deadlines for coursework and examinations to be extended on a timely written application, for instance for students with children or with dependants requiring care.

Disability and chronic illness

If a disability or a chronic illness makes it harder for you to take an assessment in the form it is set, you are entitled to compensation that restores equal opportunity, under section 6 of the examination regulations. Applications are made in writing and in good time, and applications on grounds of disability or chronic illness go to the university’s representative for students with disabilities or chronic illness.

Tip

Talk to me early, and tell me only what you want to. The formal route runs through the representative and the examination board, and I do not need to know the nature of your condition. What helps me is knowing early that an adjustment is coming, so it can be built in rather than improvised.

Language

The seminar is taught in English and the readings are in English. The term paper may be written in English or in German.

Mental health support

Life and study at the University of Mannheim can sometimes feel complicated, challenging, or overwhelming. That is completely normal.

If you ever feel mentally or emotionally strained, you may find useful advice on the university’s good mental health and studying page. The help in times of crisis page gives an overview of the counselling and support services available at the university.

AI policy

You may use AI assistance, and for some things it is genuinely useful: finding literature, checking whether an argument holds together, working on your English.

Two conditions.

Declare it. Your term paper carries a short note saying what you used and what you used it for. A sentence is enough. An undeclared use is a problem in a way that a declared one is not.

It does not make the argument. The claim, the reasoning and the judgement about what the evidence supports have to be yours, because they are what is being assessed. A paper that reads as though nobody decided anything is a weak paper whether or not a model wrote it, and you are answerable for every sentence you submit, including any citation that turns out not to exist.

Session plan

What each session covers, what you should be able to do afterwards, and what to read. Dates are on the Schedule.

Note

Session 1: Introduction and outlook

09 September 2026

What the seminar asks, how the term is organised, and what a term paper in it looks like. We also settle the schedule for the term paper pitches.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Describe what the seminar covers and how the three substantive blocks build on one another.
  • State what the term paper asks for, when the pitch happens, and what makes a question workable.

Optional

  • Torcal and Harteveld (2025)

Session 2: What is identity?

16 September 2026 · Concepts

Before we can ask what divides a democracy, we need a concept of the groups that do the dividing. This session treats identity as a demand for recognition and dignity, and asks how an attachment to a group becomes a political fact rather than a private one. Read chapters 1 and 2 of Fukuyama.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Trace where the modern concept of identity comes from and how it entered political argument.
  • Explain how a demand for recognition becomes a political demand.
  • Distinguish an identity from a preference, and say why the distinction matters for the rest of the term.

Core reading

  • Fukuyama (2018)

Optional

  • Huddy (2013)
  • Huddy (2001)
  • Westphal (2025)

Session 3: What is political polarization?

23 September 2026 · Concepts

Polarization is a metaphor borrowed from the natural sciences, and political science has attached it to several different things: how far apart elites stand, how far apart voters stand, how tightly issue positions bundle together, and how far apart the camps feel. This session sorts them apart and asks which disputes in the literature are empirical and which are definitional.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Distinguish elite from mass polarization, and ideological divergence from issue alignment.
  • Explain why two authors can disagree about whether a public is polarized without disagreeing about any fact.
  • Identify which concept of polarization a given empirical claim depends on.

Core reading

  • Abramowitz and Saunders (2008)
  • Fiorina and Abrams (2008)

Optional

  • Lelkes (2016)
  • Baldassarri and Gelman (2008)
  • McCoy and Somer (2018)
  • Turkenburg and Erkel (2025)

Session 4: What is affective polarization?

30 September 2026 · Concepts

Affective polarization (AP) is the tendency of people who identify with a party to view opposing partisans negatively and copartisans positively (Iyengar and Westwood 2015; Iyengar et al. 2019). This session takes the concept apart: the social identity logic it rests on, what it claims to add to an ideological account of division, and what has to be respecified when it travels from the two-party United States to a European multiparty system.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Derive in-group favouritism and out-group hostility from social identity theory.
  • Explain what affective polarization claims to add to an ideological account of division.
  • Identify what breaks, and what has to be respecified, when the concept moves to a multiparty system.

Core reading

  • Iyengar and Westwood (2015)
  • Wagner (2024)

Optional

  • Iyengar et al. (2012)
  • Iyengar et al. (2019)
  • Tajfel and Turner (1986)
  • Reiljan (2020)
  • Iyengar and Wagner (2025)

Session 5: Measuring affective polarization

07 October 2026 · Measurement

Almost everything we know about AP rests on one class of survey item: feeling thermometers and like/dislike ratings. This session asks what that item captures, how single ratings are aggregated into one score when there are more than two parties, and how those construction choices move the estimates and the conclusions that rest on them.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Say what a feeling thermometer measures and what it does not.
  • Reconstruct how an AP score is built in a multiparty system, and state what each step of the construction assumes.
  • Explain why the decision about who counts as a partisan changes the population an estimate refers to.
  • Judge whether a published estimate supports a comparison across countries or over time.

Core reading

  • Druckman and Levendusky (2019)
  • Wagner (2021)

Optional

  • Gidron et al. (2022)
  • Tyler and Iyengar (2024)
  • Tichelbaecker et al. (2023)
  • Kekkonen and Ylä-Anttila (2021)
  • Torcal and Comellas (2025)
  • Carlin and Love (2025)

Session 6: Beyond the thermometer: behavioral measures

14 October 2026 · Measurement

An attitude scale asks people what they feel. A behavioral measure asks them to do something: to divide money, to extend trust, to choose between candidates. We work through conjoint allocation games fielded across EU member states, and ask what a behavioral measure buys and what it costs.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Describe how a conjoint allocation game turns a choice into a measure of partisan animosity.
  • Compare behavioral and attitudinal measures on what each can and cannot support.
  • Explain why coalition partnership changes the pattern of animosity in European party systems.

Core reading

  • Hahm et al. (2024)
  • Stoetzer et al. (2023)

Optional

  • Westwood et al. (2018)
  • Carlin and Love (2013)
  • Wagner and Praprotnik (2024)
  • Röllicke (2025)

Session 7: Beyond party: social sorting and non-partisan identities

21 October 2026 · Extensions and consequences

Party is not the only line people sort along. This session asks what happens when party, region, religion, ethnicity and referendum camps line up with one another, and what happens when they cut across instead.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Define social sorting and explain why an aligned cleavage structure raises out-group animus.
  • Give a case in which the polarizing identity is not partisan at all, and say what makes it behave like one.
  • Assess how much support the evidence gives the claim that cross-cutting identities dampen animus.

Core reading

  • Harteveld (2021)
  • Hobolt et al. (2020)

Optional

  • Hahm et al. (2023)
  • Bradley and Chauchard (2022)
  • Balcells and Kuo (2023)
  • Bettarelli et al. (2023)
  • Roccas and Brewer (2002)
  • Mason (2016)
  • Zollinger (2024)
  • Mason and Versteegen (2025)
  • Hobolt and Tilley (2025)

Session 8: Is partisanship a social identity, or a cue about ideology?

04 November 2026 · Extensions and consequences

American work is split on what partisan animosity is actually about. One side reads it as group identity, dislike of them because they are them (party over policy). The other reads a party label as shorthand for policy positions, so what looks like identity is disagreement in disguise (policy over party). We follow the exchange and ask what evidence could settle it.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • State the party-over-policy and the policy-over-party positions and the evidence each rests on.
  • Explain why identity and categorisation are hard to separate in survey and experimental designs.
  • Specify what evidence would distinguish the two accounts, and why it is hard to obtain.

Core reading

  • Orr et al. (2023)
  • Dias and Lelkes (2022)

Optional

  • Orr and Huber (2020)
  • Dafoe et al. (2018)
  • Huddy et al. (2015)
  • Klar et al. (2018)
  • Bankert and Stone (2025)

Session 9: Consequences of affective polarization for democracy

11 November 2026 · Extensions and consequences

AP is usually introduced as a danger, to democratic norms, to accountability, to the willingness to live alongside people who vote differently. Recent work is more careful. Some of the alleged consequences do not survive better designs, and others hold only under particular conditions. This session separates what is established from what is asserted.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Reconstruct the argument for why AP is supposed to harm democracy.
  • Name the consequences that have actually been studied, and the designs used to study them.
  • Distinguish findings that hold up from findings that depend on context or on measurement.

Core reading

  • Harteveld et al. (2026)
  • Broockman et al. (2023)

Optional

  • Voelkel et al. (2023)
  • Versteegen et al. (2026)
  • Kekkonen and Harteveld (2025)
  • Somer and McCoy (2025)

Session 10: Open session

18 November 2026 · Extensions and consequences

This slot is deliberately unallocated. We settle the topic and the reading in the session before, so it can follow whatever the group most wants to pursue, whether that is a theme from earlier in the term or something none of the readings covered. Bring suggestions.

Afterwards you will be able to:

  • Propose a topic or a reading, and say what the seminar gains from it.
  • Read a text without a prepared framing, and take part in a discussion the group has shaped itself.
